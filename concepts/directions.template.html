Ten ways the next site could look.

Every concept below uses the same business, the same photos and the same copy as the live page, so the only thing changing is the design. Each one borrows its structure from a reference in the Inspiration Library taken from a different industry: hardware stores, architecture studios, fintech dashboards, med spas, beverage brands and more. Each one also states its motion tier under our motion rules and the main way it could fail.

The mocks are live. Hover, drag, click the hotspots and type in the boxes. Under each one, "The risk" is the main reason it could lose leads.

The idea

The hero works as an inspection. Visitors point at the kind of damage they have and learn which service fixes it and how long it takes. The service section and the form's "What does the car need?" step become one interaction.

Motion

Tier 1 · vanilla JS · 0 KB

Markers pulse quietly. The coordinate readout tracks the pointer. A click opens the service card. Nothing moves near the headline.

Use it when

Visitors don't know what to ask for ("is this a dent or a respray?"). Teaching them is what the page is for.

The risk

A hotspot only works if people tap it. If they don't, the page has a hidden menu. The hotspot answer has to carry a Get a quote button that carries the chosen service into the form.

The idea

Treat one service like a product launch. A carbon sample turns slowly in light so the gloss shifts across the weave, the way a beverage brand turns its can. It works as a landing page for one service or one ad, not as the homepage.

Motion

Tier 2 · signature · CSS 3D

The turning tile is the page's one signature moment. A pre-rendered video of the real hood would sell harder; use HyperFrames for that, at 2.5 MB or less, with a poster frame.

Use it when

You run an ad for one service ("faded carbon?") and send the clicks to a page about only that.

The risk

It's the prettiest concept and says the least. A pattern made in CSS is not proof of work. Without a real before and after of a carbon part, it's decoration.

The idea

Sell the material instead of the car. The whole hero is metallic paint shown up close, and the flake answers the cursor like real paint under a shop light. It's how property developers sell sand and stone, applied to paint.

Motion

Tier 2 · signature · canvas

The flake reacts to the pointer on desktop and drifts slowly on phones. It pauses offscreen and freezes under reduced motion.

Use it when

Brand and referral traffic from people who already know the name, or a collector-car audience that buys on taste.

The risk

There's no car, no service and no proof on the first screen. A cold visitor from Google can't tell what you do in 3 seconds. It suits an about or brand page, not a lead page.

The idea

Borrow how pre-order stores sell scarce products. A pinned card puts the offer, the availability and one red button together. Bays are limited, and the page says so.

Motion

Tier 1 · CSS · 0 KB

Only the reserve button breathes, and the card stays pinned as the page scrolls. It's calm on purpose so the red button is the only thing that moves.

Use it when

The shop really is booked out and can say how far ahead. Then scarcity is a fact you're reporting.

The risk

A fake "2 slots left" is a lie, and luxury owners notice. The bay date has to be real and kept current every week. If the shop won't maintain it, drop the row and the concept falls apart.

Sell the experience after the car is dropped off. A payments dashboard puts the exception first, and here the top line is today's update on the owner's car. The homepage shows this as a preview with the label "this is what you'll get."

Motion

Tier 1 · CSS · 0 KB

The stage bars fill once when they scroll into view. That's all. A dashboard that moves too much feels unreliable.

The risk

The idea

Treat the shop like a famous craftsman. A creative director's portfolio puts the name first and the person second. Here the name fills the screen and the car stands in for the portrait. It's editorial, black and white, and confident.

Motion

Tier 2 · signature · CSS

An ink shape drifts across the letters and inverts them, and it follows the cursor on hover. It pauses offscreen and stays still under reduced motion.

Use it when

The owner or lead tech is the brand and has a following on Instagram or YouTube. Pair it with a real portrait of that person.

The risk

A big name only works if people know it. Nobody searches "Auto Aesthetics" yet. With cold traffic it spends the first screen on a word instead of an offer.

The idea

Make the first input the whole hero. AI tools have trained everyone to type into a box, so the quote starts with a sentence, not a form. It pre-fills the vehicle and service steps behind it. Try it: type "door ding" or tap a chip.

Motion

Tier 1 · vanilla JS · 0 KB

The example text in the box rotates, the glow is static, and the make strip scrolls slowly at the bottom, away from the box, and pauses on hover.

Use it when

Mobile paid traffic, where the 3-step form is the drop-off point. A box that looks like a text message feels like no effort.

The risk

Free text is messy data, and GHL needs year, make, model and service. Keep the structured steps after the box, pre-filled. A/B test it against the current form before you commit; this is a hypothesis, not a known win.

The idea

Med spas and cosmetic dentists sell on one thing: the before and after. Cosmetic car repair is the same purchase. The comparison slider is the hero, and the italic serif sets a calm, high-end tone around it.

Motion

Tier 2 · signature · vanilla JS

The drag is the signature moment, and the visitor controls it, so it never gets in the way. It works with the keyboard (arrow keys) and needs no library.

Use it when

Almost always, as long as the shop shoots real pairs from the same angle and in the same light. It's the strongest proof format for this business.

The risk

Everything depends on the photos. Mismatched angles or lighting look faked and kill trust. The shop has one "before" photo today, so a photo-shoot process has to come first.

My call

Build 01 as the frame, 10 as the proof, 02 as the services. Test 08.

These ten were meant to be wide, not ten equally good options. Ranked on what gets a luxury owner to send the quote form:

Strongest

  • 10 Before / After. Proof is the product.
  • 02 Correction Sheet. It answers "which service do I need?"
  • 01 Showroom Noir. Safe, fast, credible.

Test, don't assume

  • 08 Describe It. A possible fix for form friction. A/B test it.
  • 06 Job Tracker. A strong difference, if ops will run it.
  • 05 Reserve the Bay. Only if the scarcity is real.

Brand pages, not lead pages

  • 03 The Weave. A one-service ad landing page.
  • 04 Macro Finish. An about page.
  • 07 Wordmark. Once the name is known.
  • 09 Ledger. For a garage-culture client.

The uncomfortable part. None of these fixes what the repo's own README calls the biggest conversion gap: the page has no phone number, address, hours or reviews. A redesign without them is a nicer-looking version of the same leak. The second blocker is photography. Every photo is a vertical phone still, and the three strongest concepts need landscape shots and matched before/after pairs. Fix those two things and the design choice matters much less.

On motion: the rules allow one signature moment per page, so a combined build picks one, the before/after drag. The hotspots and the light sweep drop to tier-1 accents.
